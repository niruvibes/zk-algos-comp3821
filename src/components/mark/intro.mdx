# What is a Zero-Knowledge Proof?
Let’s say you want to have access to sensitive data, like your banking details, 
but in order for the bank to grant you access they require a proof of 
identity from you, like a passport for example. This raises privacy and security 
concerns as personal identifiable information (PII) is also sensitive and should 
not be casually given out. 

With this issue, the concept of zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) was introduced and was 
a breakthrough in applied cryptography as it promised to improve security of 
information by removing the need to reveal information in order to prove that a 
claim is valid. ZKPs involve a prover and a verifier. The prover has a witness, 
the secret information that they claim to have, which is your PII in this case.

The aim for the prover is to convince the verifier that they have the witness 
without actually revealing the information itself (or while revealing as few 
details as possible). So by using a ZKP, you can now prove your identity without the 
need to provide your PII!

## Properties
All zero-knowledge proofs must have the following three characteristics:
- <b>Zero-knowledge:</b> no brainer, it is in the name after all. Zero-knowledge means 
that the verifier knows the validity of the statement without knowing the contents of the statement itself.
- <b>Proof of knowledge:</b> The prover must know the
witness, otherwise it is impossible for them to form
a proof with the correct logic
- <b>Completeness:</b> if the statement is true, then the verifier can trust that the prover has knowledge.
- <b>Soundness:</b> if the prover is dishonest, they cannot trick the verifier into believing their statement.

## The Process
Let Xavier be the verifier and Yasmina be the prover because we didn’t like Alice and Bob anyways.
1. Yasmina obtains the secret information which can be through her own means or provided by a third party.
2. Xavier requests Yasmina proof that she has the secret information.
3. Yasmina wants to fulfil Xavier’s request, however she wants to leak as little data as possible about the secret to him. So, she constructs a zero-knowledge proof and computes an algorithm.
4. Yasmina responds to Xavier’s request with her completed zero-knowledge proof.
5. Xavier uses Yasmina’s proof to verify that she has the correct information.

# Interactive vs. Non-Interactive ZKPs
A ZKP is said to be interactive if it requires several rounds of back and forth communication before
the verifier chooses to accept or reject the prover's claim. On the other hand, non-interactive ZKPs
exist, where the prover only needs to publish a proof once which the verifier can verify without further
communication.

## Graph 3-Colouring ZKP
The prover is given a graph, $G=(V,E)$, that they claim to be 3-colourable, 
meaning that for any vertex $V$ that is labelled a certain colour, any neighbouring 
vertices would not be of the same colour as $V$. The prover has to convince the verifier 
that the graph is 3-colourable without revealing the actual colouring of $G$ using the following method:
1. The prover, $P$, has access to a 3-colouring configuration, $\overrightarrow\sigma$, 
for $G$, randomly chooses permutation $\pi\leftarrow perm\{0,1,2\}$, where $\{0,1,2\}$ 
represents the set of colours, each labeled by a number, and commits to $\overrightarrow\sigma$.
2. A commitment scheme is made such that for every vertex $v\in V$ there exists 
$c_v$ which is the result a hash function with the inputs $\pi(\sigma_v)$, the permuted colour 
of the vertex, and $r_v$, a random key, and the prover sends the committed values 
to the verifier. To find out more about commitment schemes, go to <a href="commit">this page</a>.
3. The verifier chooses a random edge, $(i,j)\in E$, and sends the chosen edge to $P$.
4. $P$ reveals to verifier permuted colours of vertices $i$ and $j$, $\pi(\sigma_i)$ 
and $\pi(\sigma_j)$ respectively and random keys, $r_i$ and $r_j$, used to commit the colours.
5. Verifier checks if commitments match revealed values, and $\pi(\sigma_i)$ and 
$\pi(\sigma_j)$ are different. If both conditions hold there is trust.
