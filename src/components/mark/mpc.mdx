## Background
The RSA cryptosystem is one of the oldest widely used system of cryptography algorithms used
for secure data transmission. However concerns on the theoretical ability of quantum computers
to factor large numbers in polynomial time, thereby breaking the security of RSA, led to the search 
for alternative algorithms.
<br />
In 2017, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology launched the post-quantum cryptography 
project, calling for submissions of various cryptographic algorithms which would be resistant to quantum
attacks. While stronger and more efficient schemes would eventually be chosen for the standard, many of 
the submitted algorithms (PICNIC, AIMer, Biscuit,...) used schemes based on multi-party computation in 
the head, which we explore here.
<br />

# Multi-Party Computation
Multi-Party Computation (MPC) is a family of protocols whose aim is to let a collection of $n$ parties
carry out some computation. Namely each party has some input $w_i$ which they would like to keep
private, and have knowledge of public information $x$. Together they wish to compute
some **functionality**
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$f(w_1,\dots,w_n, x)$$
</div>
without revealing their private inputs.

These protocols consist of (possibly many) rounds of private computation, and communication between
the parties, with the final result being the computation of the value of $f(w_1,\dots,w_n, x)$.
Specifically, on the $i$th round, the $k$th party performs a computation:
- $\Pi_{k,i}(w_k, x,\text{prior messages recieved})=\text{next messages to send}$
- Then sends out messages to the $l$th party $m_{k,l,i}$

- The protocol is said to maintain privacy if the messages sent to a party does not reveal any
information about other parties private inputs.
<br />

    <img src="/zk-algos-comp3821/MPC_diagram.png" />
</div>
<br />
For the purpose of working towards MPC in the Head, we often consider the private inputs $w_i$ to be
shares of some secret (or a collection of shares of several secrets). 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$[w]_i=([a]_i,[b]_i,\dots)$$
</div>
Lets take the example of MPC protocols which work upon additive secret shares over a prime order finite 
field $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$.
- Other MPC protocols exist which work for other secret sharing schemes, for example 
Ben-Or-Goldwasser-Widgerson (BGW) protocols are certain MPC protocols designed around Shamir secret
shares.
- Additionally many MPC protocols exist for computation of non-prime order finite fields, boolean values and 
logical gates, matrix arithmetic and more.

Also for simplicity lets assume the functionality $f$ can be broken down into several basic arithmetic
operations, exploring some methods for their computation.
<br />
## Linear operations
### Addition of secrets and public values
Given two secrets $a,b$ with a collection of shares $[a]_i,[b]_i$ which each party holds, notice that
if each party can compute a valid secret sharing of the sum $a+b$ by adding the individual shares
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$[a+b]_i=[a]_i+[b]_i$$
</div>
It is not hard to see that indeed
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$\sum_i[a+b]_i=\sum_i[a]_i+\sum_i[b]_i=a+b$$
</div>

Now to add a public constant $x_0$ to a secret $a$, one can either choose one party (say the first)
to add $x_0$ to their share, or have all $n$ parties add $x_0\cdot n^{-1}$ to their share (where 
$n^{-1}$ is the appropriate multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$).
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$[a+x_0]_i=[a]_i+x_0\cdot n^{-1}$$
</div>
Both these operations require no communication rounds.
<br />
### Scaling by public values
Multiplication of two secrets from their shares is typically a lot more difficult, however 
multiplication of a secret $a$ by a public value $x_0$ just involves multiplying every
share by $x_0$:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$[x_0\cdot a]_i=x_0\cdot [a]_i$$
</div>
<br />
## Multiplication of secret values with Replicated Secret Sharing
{/* Mention something on beaver triples as an alternative */}
The difficulty of multiplication in additive secret sharing comes from the need to calculate
all the products of the form $[a]_i[b]_j$:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$a\cdot b=\sum_{i,j}[a]_i[b]_j$$
</div>
There are several subprotocols to carry out the multiplication of two secrets from their
shares. We will take a look at an example called **replicated secret sharing**, though
many alternatives with different tradoffs exist, for example Beaver triples.
<br />
A naive but evidently insecure version of this plays out as so; say we have five parties, which
we colour code <span style={{ color:'red' }}>Alice (1)</span>,
<span style={{ color:'orange' }}>Bob (2)</span>,
<span style={{ color:'green' }}>Cole (3)</span>,
<span style={{ color:'blue' }}>Dahlia (4)</span>,
<span style={{ color:'purple' }}>Ellie (5)</span>,
then each is tasked to compute the following products of their shares in this following manner:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$\begin{matrix} \red{[a]_1[b]_1} & \red{[a]_1[b]_2} & \red{[a]_1[b]_3} & \blue{[a]_1[b]_4} & \purple{[a]_1[b]_5} \\ \red{[a]_2[b]_1} & \orange{[a]_2[b]_2} & \orange{[a]_2[b]_3} & \orange{[a]_2[b]_4} & \purple{[a]_2[b]_5} \\ \red{[a]_3[b]_1} & \orange{[a]_3[b]_2} & \green{[a]_3[b]_3} & \green{[a]_3[b]_4} & \green{[a]_3[b]_5} \\ \blue{[a]_4[b]_1} & \orange{[a]_4[b]_2} & \green{[a]_4[b]_3} & \blue{[a]_4[b]_4} & \blue{[a]_4[b]_5} \\ \purple{[a]_5[b]_1} & \purple{[a]_5[b]_2} & \green{[a]_5[b]_3} & \blue{[a]_5[b]_4} & \purple{[a]_5[b]_5} \\ \end{matrix}$$
</div>
- Each party sends their share to $\text{ceil}(n/2)$ other parties (this step is obviously insecre!). 
For example, Dahlia sends her shares to Ellie and Alice.
- Each party then computes their products, namely Alice recieves $\red{[a]_2,[b]_2,[a]_3,[b]_3}$. from Bob 
and Cole, and calculates
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$\red{[a\cdot b]_1=[a]_1[b]_1+[a]_2[b]_1+[a]_3[b]_1+[a]_1[b]_2+[a]_1[b]_3}.$$
</div>
Together, all parties would now have computed all the share products $[a]_i[b]_j$, and so their sum
is equal to $a\cdot b$:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$a\cdot b=\sum_i [a\cdot b]_i.$$
</div>
Again for the goal of maintaining privacy of the other initial inputs, the original shares should not be 
known by other parties. It is best if we have a way to randomly create a new set of shares which can be
messaged to other people such that the original share is not known.
<br />
### Key/secret resharing
To create a new set of shares to the original, Each party performs their own additive secret sharing on 
their share to split it into $n$ smaller shares:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$[a]_i=\sum_j [a]_{i,j}.$$
</div>
Each party sends the $j$th share to the $j$th party only, then each party takes their new set of shares
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$[a]'_j=\sum_i [a]_{i,j}.$$
</div>
Evidently these new shares $[a]'_j$ all still add up to the original $a$
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$\sum_j[a]'_j=\sum_j\sum_i [a]_{i,j}=\sum_i[a]_i=a.$$
</div>
The parties are now safe to message these new shares without revealing information about the old shares.
<br />
Building blocks like the above can be found for other arithmetic operations, and also for operations 
in other settings, such as private evaluation of logical gates for boolean values, or evaluation of
matrix multiplications.
<br />
Such basic operations are the building blocks for complex functionalities which can be designed to be
difficult to reverse (in the sense that it takes an exceedingly long time to reverse; e.g. asymptotically 
faster than polynomial time in the input). This leads to the following application of MPC.
<br />
# Multi-Party Computation in the Head
Lets return to Xavier and Yasmina, they are both interested in the discrete logarithm problem, that is
given some numbers $b,y\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ (for large prime $p$), what is the 
$x\in (\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^\times = \mathbb Z/(p-1)\mathbb Z$ such that
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$F(x)=b^x\equiv y\quad\mod p.$$
</div>
Yasmina claims they found such a $x$, but would like to keep it a secret. To be able to prove to Xavier 
that their $x$ satisfies $b^x\equiv y$, without revealing $x$, she decides to split $x$ into 128 
additive shares $[x]_1,[x]_2,\dots,[x]_{128}$, and simulates the following multi-party computation:
- Have 128 simulated parties take the exponentials $[b^x]_i=b^{[x]_i}$ privately,
- Broadcast the values of $[b^x]_i$ to all the other parties,
- Collect all the broadcasted values of $[b^x]_i$ and multiply them together to get $y=[b^x]_1\cdots[b^x]_{128}$.

Yasmina then collates all the information each party gains at the end (say with a Merkle tree commit) into 
collections called views and sends them to Xavier, claiming it is a computation of $b^x\equiv y$ split across 
128 parties.

They tell Xavier is allowed to opens whatever 127 of the 128 views of the computation that he likes to verify
they did not cheat. Xavier happily obliges at these terms, noticing the chances of not catching Yasmina
cheating are $\frac 1{128}$.

Yasmina sends over the 127 views which Xavier chooses and the remaining data to reconstruct the original commit, 
and Xavier checks that:
- The opened views indeed correspond to the original commit,
- The output of all the parties calculations is indeed the value of $y$,
- There is no erroneous calculation or messaging done between parties which he can see.

If Xavier wants to be even more sure Yasmina is not cheating, he could request Yasmina to redo this interaction 
again, decreasing the chances of cheating without being caught exponentially.

<br />
The above procedure is an example of MPC in the head (MPCitH), a zero knowledge proof based on a multi-party 
computation. The prover would like to indicate to the verifier knowledge of some secret $x$ which when fed 
through some functionality gives a target value $y$:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$F(x)=y.$$
</div>
- This is typically chosen to be an $\mathsf{NP}$ relation; where knowing $x$, verifying $y=F(x)$ takes a
polynomial amount of time to the input size, but the task of finding a preimage $x$ knowing $y$ on
average takes superpolynomial time. 
- Common examples include hashing functions like SHA, or Posiedon; or the discrete logarithm problem similar
to our example, over a suitably 'difficult' group.

To do this, the prover will split the secret $x$ into $n$ shares and simulate a multi-party 
computation to compute $F(x)$ (hence the name MPC in the head).
- The prover generates shares $[x]_i$ and then runs MPC in their head to compute $F(x)$ from the split
of shares. While doing this, they collate **views** of each party $V_1,\dots V_n$. Each view $V_i$ 
consists of the $i$th party's private input, records of their computations and records of messages sent 
and recieved.
- The prover sends commits of these views $\text{Com}(V_1,\dots,V_n)$ to the verifier.
- The verifier requests for the prover to open $t$ of the parties views ${i_1,\dots, i_t}$.
- The prover opens those parties views $\text{Com}(V_{i_1},\dots,V_{i_t})$ and sends them (and any auxiliary information to verify the commitment) to the verifier.
- The verifier validates those views are consistent with the commits and that the computations are
consistent with a valid MPC computation.
- This process is repeated enough times to convince the verifier.
<br />
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
    <img src="/zk-algos-comp3821/MPCitH_diagram.png" />
</div>
<br />
This proof of knowledge is zero knowledge if the MPC protocol is private, and the secret sharing
scheme used is $t$-private where $t$ is the number of views which the verifier requests to open.

Evidently it is also complete so long as the MPC protocol is correct.

Soundness and proof of knowledge follows from the fact that a malicious prover which does not know $x$ 
must fudge the computation of $F$, visible in one of the views, which the verifier can catch if they 
open said view. Each repetition of this process makes the chance of the verifier missing this vanishingly 
small.
