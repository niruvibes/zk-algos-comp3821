# Multi-Party Computation in the Head
Lets return to Xavier and Yasmina, they are both interested in the discrete logarithm problem, that is
given some numbers $b,y\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ (for large prime $p$), what is the 
$x\in (\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^\times = \mathbb Z/(p-1)\mathbb Z$ such that
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$F(x)=b^x\equiv y\quad\mod p.$$
</div>
Yasmina claims they found such a $x$, but would like to keep it a secret. To be able to prove to Xavier 
that their $x$ satisfies $b^x\equiv y$, without revealing $x$, she decides to split $x$ into 128 
additive shares $[x]_1,[x]_2,\dots,[x]_{128}$, and simulates the following multi-party computation:
- Have 128 simulated parties take the exponentials $[b^x]_i=b^{[x]_i}$ privately,
- Broadcast the values of $[b^x]_i$ to all the other parties,
- Collect all the broadcasted values of $[b^x]_i$ and multiply them together to get $y=[b^x]_1\cdots[b^x]_{128}$.

Yasmina then collates all the information each party gains at the end (say with a Merkle tree commit) into 
collections called views and sends them to Xavier, claiming it is a computation of $b^x\equiv y$ split across 
128 parties.

They tell Xavier is allowed to opens whatever 127 of the 128 views of the computation that he likes to verify
they did not cheat. Xavier happily obliges at these terms, noticing the chances of not catching Yasmina
cheating are $\frac 1{128}$.

Yasmina sends over the 127 views which Xavier chooses and the remaining data to reconstruct the original commit, 
and Xavier checks that:
- The opened views indeed correspond to the original commit,
- The output of all the parties calculations is indeed the value of $y$,
- There is no erroneous calculation or messaging done between parties which he can see.

If Xavier wants to be even more sure Yasmina is not cheating, he could request Yasmina to redo this interaction 
again, decreasing the chances of cheating without being caught exponentially.

<br />
The above procedure is an example of MPC in the head (MPCitH), a zero knowledge proof based on a multi-party 
computation. The prover would like to indicate to the verifier knowledge of some secret $x$ which when fed 
through some functionality gives a target value $y$:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$F(x)=y.$$
</div>
- This is typically chosen to be an $\mathsf{NP}$ relation; where knowing $x$, verifying $y=F(x)$ takes a
polynomial amount of time to the input size, but the task of finding a preimage $x$ knowing $y$ on
average takes superpolynomial time. 
- Common examples include hashing functions like SHA, or Poseidon; or the discrete logarithm problem similar
to our example, over a suitably 'difficult' group.

To do this, the prover will split the secret $x$ into $n$ shares and simulate a multi-party 
computation to compute $F(x)$ (hence the name MPC in the head).
- The prover generates shares $[x]_i$ and then runs MPC in their head to compute $F(x)$ from the split
of shares. While doing this, they collate **views** of each party $V_1,\dots V_n$. Each view $V_i$ 
consists of the $i$th party's private input, records of their computations and records of messages sent 
and recieved.
- The prover sends commits of these views $\text{Com}(V_1,\dots,V_n)$ to the verifier.
- The verifier requests for the prover to open $t$ of the parties views ${i_1,\dots, i_t}$.
- The prover opens those parties views $\text{Com}(V_{i_1},\dots,V_{i_t})$ and sends them (and any auxiliary information to verify the commitment) to the verifier.
- The verifier validates those views are consistent with the commits and that the computations are
consistent with a valid MPC computation.
- This process is repeated enough times to convince the verifier.

<br />
This proof of knowledge is zero knowledge if the MPC protocol is private, and the secret sharing
scheme used is $t$-private where $t$ is the number of views which the verifier requests to open.

Evidently it is also complete so long as the MPC protocol is correct.

Soundness and proof of knowledge follows from the fact that a malicious prover which does not know $x$ 
must fudge the computation of $F$, visible in one of the views, which the verifier can catch if they 
open said view. Each repetition of this process makes the chance of the verifier missing this vanishingly 
small.
