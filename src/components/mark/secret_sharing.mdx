# Secret Sharing
**Secret sharing** refers to different schemes to distribute some secret $s$ among a group of $n$ parties, 
such that only particular subset of parties can reconstruct the secret. Namely a set of **shares** 
$[s]_1,\dots,[s]_n$ is handed out to the parties, and given knowledge of enough of them, one can determine 
what $s$ is. 
<br />
The most common type of sharing scheme is one where any group of more than $t$ party shares can reconstruct 
the secret $s$, but any group $t$ shares or less cannot determine any information on the secret. These are 
captured within the properties:
- $t$**-reconstructability** which says the algorithm for secret reconstruction is guaranteed to produce 
correct results given at least $t$ shares.
- $t$**-privacy** which says that given $t$ or less shares, one cannot learn any information about the 
secret $s$. 
<br />
## Additive Secret Sharing
Suppose Alice would like to share the password to her computer $s=196884$ to her $n=5$ other family members 
for emergency use. However, she can't trust her younger sister from snooping, or her father from keeping the 
password secure, so she picks $5$ numbers: 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
    $$\begin{aligned}[s]_1&=403,\\ [s]_2&=167606,\\ [s]_3&=68856,\\ [s]_4&=474797,\\ [s]_5&=9509,\end{aligned}$$
</div>
which add up to her password $s$ modulo her favourite large prime $p=2^{19}-1=524287$: 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
    $$[s]_1+[s]_2+[s]_3+[s]_4+[s]_5\equiv s\mod p$$
</div>
She gives one of these shares to each member of her family, and tells them "Keep these numbers safe, and in 
the case of an emergency, add all of your numbers and take the remainder dividing by $2^{19}-1$ to get my 
password".
<br />
This simple but effective example is a scheme called **additive secret sharing**. For a given secret input 
number that is contained within some large prime order field $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ (that is in the field of 
arithmetic modulo some prime $p$), we can randomly pick $n$ shares which add up to the secret.

Namely our sharing algorithm involves picking $n-1$ share numbers 
$[s]_1,\dots,[s]_{n-1}$
uniformly distributed 
across $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, and producing the last share by 
$[s]_n=s-[s]_1-\cdots-[s]_{n-1}$
.
<br />
This secret-sharing scheme is evidently $n$-reconstructable:
- One can compute the sum, which is the secret $s$, from the $n$ shares.

More subtly, this scheme is also $(n-1)$-private:
- This algorithm ensures that each share is uniformly distributed across our field $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$; 
notably the last share is uniformly distributed across our field when fixing the first $n-1$ shares and 
randomly picking the secret in some uniform distribution.
- Moreover given all but one share with known values, since the last unknown share has a value uniformly 
distributed over the field, the total sum (denoting the possibilities for the secret) are uniformly 
distributed over $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$; so if you don't have all of the shares, you are no better off than 
randomly guessing the secret.

This sharing scheme does have a disadvantage in that no redundancy is built in. If her mother loses her share, 
it is basically impossible to reconstruct Alice's password; one of the shares you are no better off than 
randomly guessing. This will not matter in our ultimate goal to construct our particular MPCitH protocol, but 
it is illuminating to to consider some alternatives.

## Shamir Secret Sharing
Alice realises the flaw in her secret sharing method, and decides to take another approach. To ensure any 
three of her family members can determine her password, she produces new shares by creating a random 
quadratic living in $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ with a y-intercept given by her password $s=196884$:
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
    $$P(x) \equiv 158246x^2 + 260818x + 196884\mod p$$
</div>
She then gives each member of her family a coordinate on this quadratic modulo $p$
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
    $$\begin{aligned}[s]_1&=(1,P(1))=(1,91661),\\ [s]_2&=(2,P(2))=(2,302930),\\ [s]_3&=(3,P(3))=(3,306404),\\ [s]_4&=(4,P(4))=(4,102083),\\ [s]_5&=(5,P(5))=(5,214254).\end{aligned}$$
</div>
All of her family which are competent in high school algebra know how to reconstruct a quadratic given three 
points, so any three of them can divulge their shares and reconstruct the y-intercept, which modulo $
p=2^{19}-1$ is Alice's password.
<br />
This is an example of **Shamir's secret sharing scheme**.

The sharing algorithm selects a degree $t-1$ polynomial 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'center' }}>
$$P(x)=a_{t-1}x^{t-1}+\cdots+a_1t^1+s,$$ 
</div>
with the secret as the constant coefficient and all other coefficients chosen randomly from a uniform 
distribution on $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, then distributes shares of the form $(i,P(i))$ for nonzero 
$i\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$.

The reconstruction algorithm uses Lagrange's polynomial interpolation formula over $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ to 
reconstruct the degree $t-1$ polynomial given $t$ of it's points.

This algorithm is $t$-reconstructable:
- Generally Lagrange's interpolation formula (which works over any field including $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$) 
guarantees that given $t$ such points with different x coordinates, one can find the unique polynomial of 
degree at most $t-1$ passing through those points.
- The primeness of $p$ matters here as otherwise the 'divisions' required in said formula do not exist in
$\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$.

Furthermore it is $(t-1)$-private:
- The reasoning for this is similar to the case of additive secret sharing.
- The primeness of $p$ matters as otherwise, given knowledge of $t-1$ of the shares, one can construct a
linear relation of the form $s=k_1a_1+k_2a_2+\cdots +k_{t-1}a_{t-1}+c$ for some $k_i,c\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ 
which could narrow down the space of possible values for $s$ unless we work modulo a prime $p$.
