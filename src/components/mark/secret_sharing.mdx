# Secret Sharing Schemes
**Secret sharing** refers to different schemes to distribute some secret $s$ among a group of $n$ parties, 
such that only particular subset of parties can reconstruct the secret. Namely a set of **shares** $[s]_1,\dots,[s]_n$
is handed out to the parties, and given knowledge of enough of them, one can determine what $s$ is. 

The most common type of sharing scheme is one where any group of more than $t$ party shares can reconstruct the secret $s$, 
but any group $t$ shares or less cannot determine any information on the secret. These are captured within the
properties:
- $t$**-reconstructability** which says the algorithm for secret reconstruction is guaranteed to produce correct results given more than $t$ shares.
- $t$**-privacy** which says that given $t$ or less shares, one cannot learn any information about the secret $s$.

## Additive Secret Sharing
Suppose Alice would like to share the password to her computer $s=196884$ to her $n=5$ other family members for emergency use. 
However, she can't trust her younger sister from snooping, or her father from keeping the password secure, so she decides 
to pick $5$ numbers: 
$$[s]_1=403,[s]_2=167606,[s]_3=68856,[s]_4=474797,[s]_5=9509,$$
which add up to her password $s$ modulo her favorite large prime $p=2^{19}-1=524287$: 
$$[s]_1+[s]_2+[s]_3+[s]_4+[s]_5\equiv s\mod p$$
She gives one of these shares to each 
member of her family, and tells them "Keep these numbers safe, and in the case of an emergency, add all of your numbers and 
take the remainder dividing by $2^{19}-1$ to get my password".

This simple but effective example is a scheme called **additive secret sharing**. For a given secret input number in some 
large prime order field $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ (that is in the field of arithmetic modulo some prime $p$), we can randomly 
pick $n$ shares which add up to the secret.

Namely our sharing algorithm involves picking $n-1$ share numbers 
$[s]_1,\dots,[s]_{n-1}$
uniformly distributed 
across $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, and producing the last share by 
$[s]_n=s-[s]_1-\cdots-[s]_{n-1}$
.

This sharing scheme does have a disadvantage in that no redundancy is built in. If her mother loses her share, it is 
basically impossible to reconstruct Alice's password; one of the shares you are no better off than randomly guessing.
This will turn out not to matter for the purposes of constructing the MPC in the head protocol, but other secret sharing
can address this problem by having a variable privacy threshold.

## Shamir Secret Sharing
Alice realises the flaw in her secret sharing method, and decides to take another approach. To ensure any three of her family
members can determine her password, she produces new shares by creating a random quadratic living in $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ with 
a y-intercept given by her password $s=196884$:

$$P(x) \equiv 158246x^2 + 260818x + 196884\mod p$$

She then gives each member of her family a coordinate on this quadratic modulo $p$
$$[s]_1=(1,P(1))=(1,91661),[s]_2=(2,P(2))=(2,302930),[s]_3=(3,P(3))=(3,306404),[s]_4=(4,P(4))=(4,102083),[s]_5=(5,P(5))=(5,214254),$$

All of her family which are competent in high school algebra know how to reconstruct a quadratic given three points, so
any three of them can divulge their shares and reconstruct the y-intercept, which modulo $p=2^\{19\}-1$ is Alice's password.

Generally Lagrange's interpolation formula (which works over any field including $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$) guarantees that given 
$t$ such points with different x coordinates, one can find the unique polynomial of degree at most $t-1$ passing through those 
points.
